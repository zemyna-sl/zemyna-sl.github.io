Dataset Overview¶

Immediately after the initial cleanup and overview, some aspects about the dataset became clear. This dataset includes responses to mental health surveys conducted in the tech industry during the years 2014, 2016, 2017, 2018, and 2019 (years corresponding to SurveyIDs and Descriptions). The data was originally provided as an SQLite database and consists of three joined tables. Here is more information about the data (based on the initial overview and the information provided when acquiring the dataset):

  • Total rows (records): 236,898
  • Total unique survey questions: 105
  • Total unique user IDs: 4218
  • User responses include multiple rows per user
  • I will be retaining missing or blank responses, as non-response is itself meaningful in survey analysis.

Gender¶

Gender is an important sociodemographic factor in this analysis, especially when evaluating how different groups may experience or report mental health conditions. In this section, I extract responses related to gender and begin normalizing the wide range of user-submitted values.

Since the gender field was open-ended, many respondents entered variations or typos (e.g., "malr", "cis-female"). To ensure consistency, these are grouped into standardized categories - Male, Female and Other (including non-binary, unspecified, or unclear responses).

This cleanup allows for meaningful comparisons in later stages of the analysis.

In [18]:
gender_df = df[
    df["QuestionText"].str.lower().str.contains("your gender", na=False)
].copy()


def map_gender(g):
    if not isinstance(g, str):
        return "Other"

    g_lower = g.strip().lower()

    if g_lower in [
        "male",
        "m",
        "man",
        "cis male",
        "cis-male",
        "male-ish",
        "maile",
        "malr",
        "mail",
    ]:
        return "Male"
    elif g_lower in [
        "female",
        "f",
        "woman",
        "cis female",
        "cis-female",
        "femake",
        "female (cis)",
        "femail",
    ]:
        return "Female"
    else:
        return "Other"

Prevalence Rates and Confidence Intervals¶

After the initial overview of the 3 questions, I will now take a more statistical approach.

To better understand how these rates might generalize beyond our sample, I calculated the prevalence rate (i.e., percentage of yes answers) along with a 95% confidence interval (CI) for each of the three key mental health questions:

In [32]:
mh_questions = [
    "Do you currently have a mental health disorder?",
    "Have you had a mental health disorder in the past?",
    "Have you ever been diagnosed with a mental health disorder?",
]

results = []

for question in mh_questions:
    subset = df[df["QuestionText"] == question].copy()

    subset["CleanAnswer"] = subset["AnswerText"].apply(
        lambda x: "Yes" if x == "Yes" else ("No" if x == "No" else "Uncertain")
    )

    total = subset.shape[0]
    yes_count = (subset["CleanAnswer"] == "Yes").sum()
    proportion = yes_count / total

    ci_low, ci_high = proportion_confint(yes_count, total, alpha=0.05, method="wilson")

    results.append(
        {
            "Question": question,
            "Yes (%)": proportion * 100,
            "CI Lower": ci_low * 100,
            "CI Upper": ci_high * 100,
        }
    )

mh_ci_df = pd.DataFrame(results)

The confidence intervals around these estimates are quite narrow (approximately 2–3%), likely influenced by the relatively large sample sizes.

Overall, this snapshot confirms that mental health concerns are highly prevalent among tech industry professionals, whether currently ongoing, previously experienced, or clinically diagnosed. Conclusion¶

In this project, I analyzed the Mental Health in the Tech Industry dataset by cleaning, organizing, and statistically exploring key mental health questions using SQL, pandas, and visualization tools. My main goal was to uncover meaningful patterns related to mental health status, gender, and age—and to communicate not only the findings, but also the uncertainty around them.

Some key findings that were uncovered:

  • A high percentage of survey respondents claim to have had a mental health disorder in the past (around 47%) or had a diagnosis (46%). This shows that a large portion of individuals in the tech field have experienced mental health challenges.
  • Female respondents reported a higher rate of current mental health disorders (≈47%) than male respondents (≈34%) in the analyzed sample.
  • Confidence intervals provided clarity. By calculating 95% confidence intervals for all major proportions, I was able to visualize the potential range in which the true population values lie. This added transparency and reminded us not to overinterpret small differences, especially where sample sizes varied.
  • In terms of Mental health insights related to age, the 25–44 age groups offered the most stable and reliable insights due to larger sample sizes.

The dataset provides a lot of freedom to explore. A more in-depth analysis could be performed in the future, focusing on a variety of features and their correlation.
